AI systems take exams. This catches the ones that didn't really study.

Free · open source · 30-second self-check · technical version here
If you have 30 seconds

AI tools that fetch information — search engines, recommendation systems, the part of ChatGPT that finds source articles — are graded on how often they return the right document for a given question. Some of them get a high grade not by understanding the question, but by exploiting which answers happen to be most popular in the test. We built a 30-second check that spots that pattern. It's free, open-source, and works on any AI fetcher you point it at.

If you have 2 minutes — the school story

How a student who didn't study can score 80%.

Imagine you give a hundred students a multiple-choice exam. Most questions are about French history. Roughly 80% of the correct answers happen to be "C" — maybe the teacher who wrote the test had a habit. It happens.

One student, Mira, didn't study. She doesn't speak French. She doesn't know what Napoleon did. But she figured out from looking at past tests that "C" is the most common answer. So she just writes C for every single question. Mira scores 80%.

If you only see her score, you'll think Mira is a French history expert. She isn't. She figured out the test, not the subject.

This is happening to a lot of AI systems right now. Their published scores look brilliant. Some of them are doing the AI equivalent of "always pick C."

Why this matters in the real world

  • Resume screening AI that "matches the right candidate 87% of the time" — sometimes really just recommending people from the three universities most past hires came from, regardless of what the job needs.
  • Medical search tools claiming to find "the most relevant paper" — sometimes just returning the most-cited paper in that specialty.
  • The retrieval part of ChatGPT ("here are my sources") — sometimes just returning the most popular document in the database.

In all three cases, the system looks like it's reasoning. It's actually exploiting that some answers are popular. Nobody can tell from the score alone.

If you have 5 minutes — try it yourself

Four imaginary students. The exam tells you which one your AI is.

Our tool gives the same exam to four imaginary students who can't possibly know the real subject, then compares the AI's score to theirs:

  1. Imaginary Student A — "Shuffled answers." Takes the answer key and randomly rearranges it. Beating this means the AI isn't just memorising answer positions.
  2. Imaginary Student B — "Roll a dice." Random for each question. Beating this means the AI isn't just guessing.
  3. Imaginary Student C — "Random multi-pick." Picks five random answers per question. Beating this means the AI's narrowing-down is meaningful.
  4. Imaginary Student D — "Mira." Always picks the most common answer. Beating this means the AI is actually using the question — not exploiting popularity. This one is the new contribution. Most published AI evaluations forget about it.

If you have 8 minutes — see real numbers

What it actually says when you point it at a real engine.

The tool was developed and validated on a real Sanskrit-and-Tamil literary search engine over 10,000 test questions. Here are the actual numbers from the v0.1 release. Each row is a different "student" graded by all four imaginary tests:

Student typeExam scorevs Avs Bvs Cvs MiraVerdict
Anti-oracle (always wrong)0%−25−25−25−25FAIL (correct)
Constant predictor18%+9+10−7+0FAIL on Mira
Mira-style (most popular)34%+19+19+19+0FAIL on Mira only
Real engine (the one we built)79%+55+55+55+52PASS
Oracle (perfect)100%+75+75+75+74PASS

Look at the third row. The Mira-style student gets 34% — that's positive. It beats imaginary students A, B, and C by 19 percentage points each. Without the Mira test, you'd say this student passed.

But the Mira test gives a margin of zero. The fourth check is what proves: this student isn't really studying — it's just exploiting the popular-answer pattern.

This isn't a hypothetical. The first numbers we ourselves published were inflated by a programming bug that produced exactly this pattern. We retracted them publicly. That retraction is what gave the validated numbers their weight.

If you have 12 minutes — what didn't work

Things I tried that failed (and what they teach).

I'm telling you these so you know I'm not selling a magic box. I'm selling something that's been honestly tested and has visible flaws.

1 · The fancy maths feature that turned out negative

I tried adding a sophisticated analysis technique to make the AI better. After running it on 10,000 test questions, the result was statistically significant — and slightly worse than not using it. I disabled the feature. The whole point of this kind of testing is to catch yourself when you're wrong; that was the catch.

2 · The programming bug that inflated my first numbers

A subtle Python issue meant some of my settings were silently using old values. The published numbers were higher than the real ones. When I found the bug, I retracted the inflated numbers publicly. The corrected numbers were lower but real.

3 · A friend in India broke the install in 90 seconds

Akosh tried to install my tool last week. Three things in the README were wrong. The "pip install" command pointed to a package that doesn't exist. The demo command had a typo. The clone URL was a placeholder I forgot to fill in. He got "failed basic bench tests" — exactly the kind of report that would normally mean my whole library is broken. It wasn't broken; my install instructions were. Forty-five minutes after he reported it, the fixed version was live. He's named in the project's changelog as the v0.1.2 reporter.

4 · I had a $2,000 bug bounty. I suspended it.

The original methodology paper offered $2,000 cash to anyone who could break the methodology in specific ways. After the install bugs surfaced, I suspended the bounty pending broader validation. Until the implementation has been validated by an external lab, paying for breakage when I haven't yet hardened the entry doors is the wrong order.

What this list is not

It is not a list of fatal flaws — every active research project has these. It is a list of moments where the system caught itself. That's what you're looking for in any methodology: not "did it have problems" but "did it surface them."

If you have 15 minutes — limits, prior work, who's checked it

What this tool cannot tell you, and who has actually verified it.

Five honest limits

  1. It only checks AI systems that fetch documents or rank options. Not the part of ChatGPT that writes paragraphs of text. Different problem; different test we haven't built.
  2. It can't tell you the AI is "smart" — only that it isn't faking. Green light = doing real work. Doesn't mean good enough for your specific job.
  3. You bring the questions and answers. Garbage exam, garbage verdict.
  4. It assumes test questions are independent. If half your questions are basically the same question rephrased, the tool's confidence will be too high. Known limit.
  5. It doesn't catch plagiarism, training-data leakage, or AI hallucinations. Those are different problems.

Who has independently checked this

Honest answer as of today: almost nobody, yet. The methodology paper is public. The code is public. The math is undergraduate-level statistics packaged so you don't need to be a statistician.

The independent validation step — having a major AI research lab (EleutherAI, METR, Allen Institute / AI2, HuggingFace, or Anthropic) run the tool against their own published results and confirm the methodology works — is the highest priority next step. It has not happened yet. If you're at one of those labs and reading this, that's what I most need.

What we're not the first to do

Other people have built similar things. We stand on:

  • BEIR (Thakur et al, 2021) — diversifies the bench. We diversify the imaginary students.
  • MTEB (Muennighoff et al, 2022) — leaderboard infrastructure. We are a per-system audit.
  • OLMES (AI2, 2024) — open evaluation standards. We add the missing fourth check.
  • RAGAS (Es et al, 2023) — evaluates the answer-generation side. We evaluate the document-fetching side.
  • The whole TREC tradition going back to Voorhees, 1998 — meta-evaluation as a discipline.

The contribution here is narrow and specific: the Mira-style imaginary student, packaged as a 30-second four-test gate that anyone can run. Everything else is lineage.
