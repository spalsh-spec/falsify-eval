A 7-minute plain-English explainer

Is the AI actually any good? A way to tell.

A school-test analogy that explains why a lot of "AI is amazing" claims aren't quite what they seem — and what we built to spot the fakes.

For readers who have used ChatGPT once · technical version here

1 · The school-test problem.

Imagine you give a hundred students a multiple-choice exam. The questions are mostly about French history. Roughly 80% of the correct answers happen to be "C." (Why? Maybe the teacher who wrote the test had a habit. It happens.)

Now imagine one student in the class — call her Mira — who didn't study at all. She didn't read the textbook. She doesn't speak French. But she figured out, from looking at past tests, that "C" is the most common answer. So she just writes C for every single question.

Mira scores about 80%.

If you only look at her score, you'll think Mira understands French history at a serious level. She doesn't understand anything. She figured out the test, not the subject.

This is exactly what is happening to a lot of AI systems right now. Their published scores look brilliant. Some of them are doing the AI equivalent of "always pick C."

2 · Why this matters in real life.

Three places where the "always pick C" problem is happening right now in production AI systems:

  • Resume screening. A company brags that its AI "matches the right candidate to the right job 87% of the time." Sometimes the AI has noticed that 87% of historical hires came from three universities, and it's just recommending people from those three universities — regardless of the actual job description.
  • Medical search. A doctor's AI tool says "the most relevant paper for your patient's symptoms is X" with high confidence. Sometimes it's just returning the most-cited paper in that medical category, regardless of whether the patient's specific symptoms match.
  • Web search and RAG (the system that fetches sources before ChatGPT-style answers). The AI claims it found the perfect document for your question. Sometimes it just returned the most popular document in the database, ignoring what you actually asked.

In all three cases, the system looks like it's reasoning. It's actually just exploiting that some answers are popular. The user can't tell from the score alone.

3 · What we built — four imaginary test-takers.

Our tool does something simple: it gives the same exam to four imaginary students who can't possibly know the real subject, and compares the AI's score to theirs.

  1. Imaginary student A: "Shuffle the answers." We take the actual answer key and randomly rearrange it. If the AI still beats this scrambled version by a meaningful margin, we know the AI isn't just memorising answer positions.
  2. Imaginary student B: "Roll a dice for each question." Picks completely random answers, ignoring patterns. Beating this proves the AI isn't just guessing.
  3. Imaginary student C: "Pick five answers at random for each question." Like a multiple-pick version of B. Beating this proves the AI's narrowing-down is meaningful.
  4. Imaginary student D: "Mira." The student who picks 'C' because 'C' is most common. This is the new one. This is the student most published AI evaluations forget to compare against. If the AI doesn't beat Mira, the AI is doing what Mira does: exploiting the popularity distribution, not understanding the question.

Our tool runs all four comparisons automatically. If the real AI beats all four imaginary students by a clear margin, the AI gets a green light. If it fails to beat even one — especially Mira — it gets a red flag with an explanation of which trick it was probably playing.

5 · What this tool cannot tell you.

It is not magic. Five honest limits:

  1. It only checks AI systems that "fetch documents" or "rank options." Search engines, recommendation systems, the part of ChatGPT that finds source articles. It does not check the part that writes paragraphs of text. That's a different kind of test we haven't built yet.
  2. It can't tell you the AI is "smart" — only that it isn't faking. A green light means "this AI is doing real work." It does not mean "this AI is good enough for your specific job."
  3. It needs you to bring the questions and answers. We don't decide what counts as the right answer. You do. Garbage exam, garbage verdict.
  4. It assumes your test questions are independent. If half your test questions are basically the same question rephrased, the tool's confidence will be too high. We're working on a fix.
  5. It doesn't catch plagiarism, training-data leakage, or AI lying about what it knows. Those are different problems with different tools. We're solving one specific problem.

6 · Things I tried that didn't work.

I'm telling you these because you should know I'm not selling a magic box. I'm selling something that has been honestly tested and has visible flaws.

  1. I tried adding a "fancy maths feature" called fractal analysis to make the AI better. After running it on 10,000 test questions, the result was statistically significant — and slightly worse than not using it. I disabled the feature. The whole point of this kind of testing is to catch yourself when you're wrong; that was the catch.
  2. My first published numbers were inflated by a programming bug. A subtle Python issue meant some of my settings were silently using old values. When I found the bug and fixed it, I retracted the inflated numbers publicly. The corrected numbers were lower but real.
  3. A friend in India named Akosh tried to install my tool last week and three things in the README were wrong. The "pip install" command pointed to a package that doesn't exist. The demo command had a typo. The clone URL was a placeholder I forgot to fill in. Akosh got "failed basic bench tests" — exactly the kind of report that would normally mean my whole library is broken. It wasn't broken; my install instructions were. Forty-five minutes after he reported it, the fixed version was live. He's credited in the project's changelog as the v0.1.2 reporter.
  4. I had a $2,000 cash bug bounty offered for anyone who could break the methodology. I suspended it. Until the implementation has been validated by an external lab, paying for breakage when I haven't yet hardened the entry doors is the wrong order of operations.

7 · Who has checked this so far.

Honest answer: almost nobody, yet. The methodology paper is public. The code is public. The math is undergraduate-level statistics packaged so you don't need to be a statistician to use it.

The independent validation step — having a major AI research lab (one of EleutherAI, METR, AI2, HuggingFace, or Anthropic) run the tool against their own published results and confirm the methodology works — is the highest priority next step. It has not happened yet.

If you are reading this and you are at one of those labs, that is what I most need.

8 · Why you might care even if you don't work in AI.

The AI industry is now claiming, in product marketing, healthcare deployments, hiring tools, finance, and legal services, that its systems pass certain bars of capability. Some of those claims are real. Some are the equivalent of Mira's 80%.

You — as a customer, a patient, a job applicant, or a regulator — currently have no quick way to tell which is which. The published scores don't help, because they're the scores being inflated.

What we built is an honest, free, open-source way for anyone with the technical skills to grade an AI system on whether it's really doing what it claims. It runs in 30 seconds. It is small enough to audit completely. It has caught fake-looking results in our own work, more than once.

The single biggest thing this project will not do is make AI worse. It will not slow down responsible companies. It will only make it harder for irresponsible ones to claim numbers they didn't earn.
